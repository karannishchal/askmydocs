Label noise refers to incorrect or inconsistent labels in a training dataset, and it is one of the most common causes of degraded machine learning model performance. Noisy labels can arise from human annotation error, ambiguous class boundaries, or automated labelling pipelines that make systematic mistakes. Even a small percentage of mislabeled examples can meaningfully reduce a classifier's accuracy, particularly for models that are prone to overfitting the training distribution.

Several strategies exist to correct or mitigate label noise. Confident learning identifies likely mislabeled examples by comparing a model's predicted probabilities against the given labels, flagging cases where the model is confidently wrong. Cross-validation-based label cleaning trains multiple models on different folds of the data and treats disagreement across folds as a signal of a potentially incorrect label. Loss correction techniques modify the training loss function itself to be more robust to a known or estimated noise rate.

In healthcare datasets specifically, label noise is especially consequential because incorrect labels can directly affect diagnostic accuracy. A study evaluating diabetes prediction found that correcting noisy labels using a systematic relabelling pipeline improved F1 score by a meaningful margin, and that gains from label correction were often larger than gains achieved by switching to a more complex model architecture. This suggests that for many real-world tabular and clinical datasets, data quality investment can matter more than model complexity.

Gradient boosted tree models such as XGBoost are commonly used as baselines for structured healthcare data because they handle mixed feature types well and are relatively robust to noise compared to deep learning models trained from scratch on small datasets. Deep learning approaches such as TensorFlow and Keras based neural networks can outperform tree-based models when given sufficient clean data and appropriate regularization, but they tend to be more sensitive to label noise in smaller datasets.
